Introduction

I have been writing resumes since middle school and much of the advice I was given included formatting, grammar and proper sectioning. Some would even dive deeper into the content to write. But never have I received advice about how it should be written.

A resume is often the first impression you make on a potential employer. It is the document that introduces your experience, skills, and accomplishments before you ever get the chance to speak with someone. Yet, creating a good resume is surprisingly difficult. A lot of resume advice focuses on familiar recommendations: use action verbs, quantify your impact, keep it to one page, and tailor it to the job description. While these are all useful practices, they don't necessarily answer the most important question: how do you know whether what you have written is actually good?

You can describe what you worked on, list the technologies you used, and explain your responsibilities. But does that communicate the value you brought to the team? Does it show why someone should hire you? More importantly, does it connect your experience to what the employer is looking for? A strong resume is not simply a record of everything you have done. It is a way of communicating why your experience matters. In this article, we will walk through six practical ways to make your resume stronger, communicate your accomplishments, and present your experience in a way that helps recruiters and hiring managers understand your value.

1. Use Clear, Standard Resume Sections

Before focusing on the wording of your resume, make sure the overall structure is easy to understand. Recruiters often spend very little time reviewing an individual resume. They should not have to search through paragraphs of text to figure out where you worked, what you studied, or what skills you have. A clear resume structure makes it easier for someone to quickly find the information they need. Most resumes should include the following sections:

  • Contact Information: Your name, email address, phone number, and relevant professional links such as LinkedIn or GitHub.
  • Professional Summary: A short introduction that communicates your background, experience, and the type of role you are pursuing. This section is optional, especially for candidates early in their careers.
  • Work Experience: Your previous roles, internships, and relevant professional experience.
  • Education: Your degrees, universities, graduation dates, and relevant academic achievements.
  • Skills: Technical skills, tools, programming languages, and other capabilities relevant to the position.
  • Projects: Personal, academic, or professional projects that demonstrate skills and experience not already covered in your work history.
The exact sections you include will depend on your experience and the role you are applying for. For example, a recent graduate may want to highlight projects and education, while someone with several years of professional experience may want to prioritize their work history. The goal is to make the resume easy to navigate without overwhelming the reader. Before rewriting your resume, it is useful to understand what is already working and what needs improvement. This Resume Review tool (it's free!) helps you evaluate your resume and identify areas that could be improved. This is a good starting point if you are unsure whether your bullet points communicate your accomplishments effectively, whether your experience is relevant, or whether important information is missing. Instead of immediately rewriting everything, start by understanding the gaps.

2. Write With Strong Action Verbs

One of the easiest ways to improve a resume is to pay attention to how each bullet point begins. Consider the difference between these two statements: 


Responsible for building a dashboard to monitor application performance.


Developed a performance monitoring dashboard that helped
the engineering team identify application bottlenecks.

Both statements describe similar work, but the second communicates ownership and gives the reader a better understanding of the contribution. Words such as "responsible for," "helped with," and "worked on" often describe a person's involvement without explaining what they actually accomplished. Instead, use action verbs that make your contribution clear. Some examples include:

  • Built: Created a new application, system, tool, or feature.
  • Architected: Designed the structure of a system or technical solution.
  • Automated: Reduced manual work by creating an automated process.
  • Optimized: Improved the performance, efficiency, or reliability of a process.
  • Implemented: Put a technical solution or feature into practice.
  • Led: Took ownership of a project, initiative, or team effort.
  • Analyzed: Investigated data or information to identify patterns and insights.
  • Designed: Planned and developed the approach for a solution.
However, action verbs alone will not make a resume impressive. The important thing is that the verb accurately describes your contribution. For example, do not use "architected" simply because you participated in a project. Use it when you actually made decisions about the system's design and structure. Your resume should communicate what you did, not just sound impressive.

3. Show Outcomes, Not Just Responsibilities

This is arguably one of the most important parts of writing a strong resume. A common mistake is describing a job through a list of responsibilities instead of accomplishments. For example: 


Worked on improving the performance of an application.

This tells the reader what area you worked on, but it does not explain what changed because of your work. Compare it with: 


Optimized application performance by reducing API response
times by 40%, improving the experience for thousands of users.

The second example gives the reader something concrete to evaluate. It communicates the action, the result, and the significance of the work. When writing a bullet point, think about three things:

  • Action: What did you actually do?
  • Method: How did you accomplish it?
  • Result: What changed as a result of your work?
You do not need a numerical metric for every accomplishment. Not every contribution can be measured in percentages, revenue, or time saved. Results can also include improvements in reliability, usability, maintainability, customer experience, or team productivity. For example: 


Designed and implemented an automated reporting workflow,
eliminating repetitive manual analysis for the team.

Even without a numerical metric, the result is clear. If you do have metrics, make sure they are accurate and provide meaningful context. A percentage without an explanation of what it measures may not tell the reader very much. The best bullet points make it easy to understand both the work you performed and why it mattered.

4. Tailor Your Resume to the Job Description

A resume should not be treated as a document that you write once and send to every company. Different roles can have very different expectations, even when they share the same job title. For example, consider two software engineering job descriptions. One company may prioritize backend development, distributed systems, and database design. Another may be looking for experience with machine learning, model evaluation, and AI orchestration. You might have experience in all of these areas, but the most relevant experience should be emphasized depending on the position. This does not mean inventing experience or adding keywords that do not describe your actual work. It means making deliberate choices about which parts of your background deserve the most attention. When reviewing a job description, look for:

  • Required skills: The technologies, tools, and technical capabilities the employer expects.
  • Responsibilities: The problems you would be expected to solve in the role.
  • Experience requirements: The level of ownership, leadership, and professional experience being requested.
  • Repeated terminology: Concepts or skills that appear multiple times throughout the description.
Then compare these requirements with your existing experience. For example, if a role emphasizes distributed systems, consider whether your previous work involved designing services, handling large volumes of data, improving system reliability, or building scalable infrastructure. You may already have relevant experience, but your resume might not communicate it clearly. Tailoring your resume is about making that connection visible.

One important note is that a strong resume for one position may not be the strongest resume for another. The Resume Tailor tool helps you align your resume with a specific job description. You can use it to identify relevant experience and make sure the skills and accomplishments that matter most to the role are clearly represented. The goal is to make your existing experience more relevant to the opportunity without adding skills or accomplishments you do not have.

5. Don't Have All the Experience? Build It.

One of the most frustrating parts of applying for jobs is seeing a list of requirements and feeling like you do not have enough experience. This is especially common for students, recent graduates, and people trying to transition into a new field. You might see a job description asking for experience with a particular technology, framework, or type of project. If you have never used it professionally, it can feel like you have no way to demonstrate that capability. But professional experience is not the only way to build relevant skills. You can create projects, contribute to open-source software, participate in research, build applications, or solve problems that demonstrate your ability to work with the technologies you are interested in. For example, if you are interested in backend engineering, you could build an application with a database, authentication, and a set of APIs. If you are interested in machine learning, you could build a model, evaluate its performance, and create a system that makes the results accessible to users. The important part is to go beyond simply following a tutorial. Try to understand the problem you are solving, the decisions you made, and the tradeoffs involved. When describing a project on your resume, focus on:

  • The problem you wanted to solve.
  • The technical approach you chose.
  • The tools and technologies you used.
  • The challenges you encountered.
  • The outcome of the project.
A well-developed project can demonstrate initiative, technical ability, and problem-solving skills, even if you have not yet had the opportunity to use those skills in a professional setting. You do not need to wait for someone to give you experience. You can start building it yourself.

6. Make Every Line Earn Its Place

A resume has limited space. Every bullet point, sentence, and section should contribute something meaningful. It is tempting to include every project, responsibility, technology, and accomplishment you have accumulated throughout your career. However, more information does not automatically make a resume better. A long resume can make it harder for a recruiter to identify your most relevant experience. When reviewing your resume, ask yourself:

  • Does this bullet point communicate a meaningful contribution?
  • Is this experience relevant to the role I am applying for?
  • Am I repeating information that is already communicated elsewhere?
  • Can I explain this accomplishment more clearly and concisely?
  • Does this statement demonstrate a skill, outcome, or level of ownership?
For example, consider a bullet point that describes a responsibility you performed every day but does not explain the value of that work. Instead of keeping it simply because it was part of your job, think about whether there was a larger outcome associated with it. Did it improve a process? Help customers? Reduce errors? Make a system more reliable? Enable another team to work more efficiently? If the answer is yes, rewrite the bullet to communicate that contribution. If a line does not help the reader understand your experience or suitability for the role, consider removing it. A strong resume is not the one that contains the most information. It is the one that communicates the most relevant information clearly.

The objective is not to make every sentence sound complicated or fill your resume with impressive-sounding words. It is to make your contributions easier to understand, emphasize your actions, and communicate the outcomes of your work.

How Do You Actually Know If Your Resume Is Good?

We have discussed structure, action verbs, accomplishments, tailoring, projects, and conciseness. These are all important parts of resume writing. But there is still one question that is difficult to answer: how do you know whether the content you have written is actually effective? You can follow every resume-writing recommendation and still end up with bullet points that are vague, repetitive, or disconnected from the job you want. For example, you might write: 


Implemented a machine learning solution to improve business processes.

This sounds reasonable, but it leaves several questions unanswered. What problem did the solution address? What machine learning approach did you use? What part did you personally implement? How did the solution improve the process? A resume needs to answer these questions without requiring the recruiter to guess. This is where reviewing your resume critically becomes important. You need to evaluate not only whether your statements are grammatically correct, but whether they communicate your actual experience effectively. Rather than simply focusing on rewriting sentences, the goal is to help you understand what your resume communicates and where it can be stronger.

Final Thoughts

Writing a good resume is not about finding the perfect template or using the most impressive vocabulary. It is about understanding your own experience and communicating it in a way that makes its value clear to someone who has never worked with you. Focus on what you accomplished, explain how you contributed, and show why the work mattered. Make sure your resume reflects the requirements of the roles you are pursuing, and do not be afraid to build new experience when you find gaps. Most importantly, do not assume that following a checklist automatically makes your resume good. Take the time to evaluate what you have written and ask whether it actually communicates the person and professional you are. Your resume is more than a list of things you have done. It is an opportunity to show what you can bring to the next opportunity.

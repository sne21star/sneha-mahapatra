Introduction

Writing a resume can feel like guesswork. You spend hours describing your experience, choosing action verbs, and trying to quantify your accomplishments, but how do you actually know whether what you've written is good? More importantly, how do you know whether your resume communicates what an employer is looking for? A common piece of advice is to describe what you've done, use strong action verbs, and demonstrate measurable results. While these are important, they are only part of the equation. A well-written resume isn't necessarily an effective resume for every job. The real goal of your resume is to demonstrate why you are a good fit for the specific position you're applying for. Think about it from the employer's perspective. A recruiter is trying to find the best candidate for a role. They have a job description outlining the skills, experience, and qualifications they need. Your resume should make it easy for them to recognize how your background aligns with those requirements. You shouldn't make a recruiter search through several sections to understand whether you have the skills they're looking for. Instead, your resume should communicate your most relevant qualifications clearly, accurately, and efficiently. This is the approach behind Resume Craft. We built tools to help students and professionals evaluate their resumes, identify gaps, improve their writing, tailor their experience to specific opportunities, and discover jobs that better align with their qualifications. Here are six ways to build a stronger resume and make your experience count.

Use Clear, Standard Resume Sections

A well-organized resume makes it easier for recruiters and hiring managers to understand your background. Most recruiters have limited time to review applications, so your resume should make the most important information easy to find. Start with a concise professional summary, followed by your work experience, education, and relevant skills. Depending on your background, you can also include projects, certifications, publications, or volunteer experience. The order of these sections should reflect your experience and the position you're pursuing. For example, if you're a recent graduate, education and relevant projects may deserve more attention. If you're an experienced professional, your work experience and accomplishments may be the most important sections. Think about what a recruiter needs to know first. If a job requires Python, C++, or C#, make sure your relevant technical skills are easy to identify. If the role emphasizes leadership, make sure your leadership experience isn't buried beneath unrelated information. The goal isn't to force every resume into the same template. It's to organize your qualifications so the most relevant information is immediately accessible. Write With Strong Action Verbs

The way you describe your experience can significantly influence how employers perceive your contributions. Generic phrases like "responsible for managing projects" or "worked on software development" describe your duties but don't communicate what you actually accomplished. Instead, start your bullet points with specific action verbs such as built, led, improved, analyzed, launched, implemented, or reduced. These words make your contributions more direct and help communicate your level of ownership. For example: Instead of: "Responsible for improving the team's reporting process." Try: "Streamlined the team's reporting process to improve visibility into project progress." The second version communicates a clearer action and purpose. It gives the reader a better understanding of what you contributed. However, strong action verbs alone aren't enough. Your descriptions should accurately reflect your role and explain the work you actually performed. Don't exaggerate your involvement just to make a bullet point sound impressive. Tailor Your Resume to the Job Description

This is arguably one of the most important parts of writing an effective resume. You can have years of experience, excellent technical skills, and impressive accomplishments, but if your resume doesn't communicate how those qualifications relate to the position, an employer may not recognize your potential. Consider this: a company is hiring a software engineer and specifically asks for experience with Python or C++. You have both skills, but your resume lists them in an unorganized skills section, uses different terminology, or focuses heavily on unrelated experience. The recruiter now has to spend additional time figuring out whether you meet the requirements. Your job is to make that connection clear. Start by reading the job description carefully. Identify the skills, responsibilities, technologies, and qualifications the employer emphasizes. Then compare those requirements against your own experience. For example: If the job emphasizes Python, highlight relevant Python projects and professional experience. If it emphasizes leadership, demonstrate where you've led a team, mentored others, or taken ownership of a project. If it emphasizes problem-solving, explain the problems you've solved and the approach you took. If it emphasizes communication, show examples of collaboration, presentations, or cross-functional work. Think of your resume as an answer to the job description. The employer is explaining what they need, and your resume should demonstrate how your experience addresses those needs. This doesn't mean copying the job description or adding qualifications you don't have. It means presenting your genuine experience in a way that makes its relevance obvious. A useful way to think about this is to imagine you've already been hired for the position. What experiences, skills, and accomplishments would demonstrate that you can perform the job successfully? Your resume should help the employer see that potential. Don't Have All the Experience? Build It.

One of the biggest concerns people have when applying for a job is not having enough experience. This is especially common among students, recent graduates, and people trying to transition into a new industry. But professional experience isn't the only way to demonstrate your capabilities. You can build projects, contribute to open-source work, pursue certifications, participate in research, volunteer, or develop skills independently. The important thing is to create opportunities to demonstrate that you understand the work and can apply what you've learned. For example, imagine you want to work at Spotify in a software engineering position. Instead of only listing programming languages and general software projects, consider building an application that combines music and software engineering. You could develop a music recommendation system, analyze listening patterns, or build an application that uses music data to generate personalized playlists. This gives you something concrete to discuss in an interview and something relevant to include on your resume. It demonstrates initiative, technical ability, and an interest in the industry. The same principle applies to other fields. If you're interested in finance, consider building a financial analysis project. If you're interested in product management, develop a product proposal or conduct user research. If you're interested in data science, work on a project that demonstrates your ability to analyze and interpret data. You don't have to wait for someone to give you experience. You can intentionally create opportunities to build it. The key is to connect the experience you're developing with the type of work you want to do next. Once you've built those skills, make sure your resume explains what you created, which tools you used, and what you learned or accomplished.

Make Every Line Earn Its Place

Your resume has limited space, and every section should contribute to the story you're telling about your professional background. Review each bullet point and ask yourself whether it demonstrates a relevant skill, accomplishment, or contribution. Remove repetitive descriptions, vague filler, and outdated details that no longer support your career goals. Prioritize recent and relevant experience, especially when applying for a specific position. Earlier projects, internships, leadership experiences, and transferable skills can still be valuable, but be intentional about how much space they deserve. Also pay attention to grammar, tense, and formatting. Use past tense when describing completed roles and present tense for responsibilities you currently perform. Keep dates, punctuation, spacing, capitalization, and bullet formatting consistent. A clean and readable format is important for both recruiters and applicant tracking systems. Avoid unnecessarily complicated layouts, unusual section headings, or formatting that makes your information difficult to interpret. A strong resume isn't necessarily the longest one. It's the one that communicates your qualifications clearly, efficiently, and convincingly. Final Thoughts: Your Resume Should Work for the Job You're Applying For

Writing a resume is about more than listing your education, experience, and skills. It's about understanding what an employer needs and communicating how your background can help meet those needs. A strong resume makes that connection easy to see. It highlights relevant experience, demonstrates your accomplishments, uses language that reflects the role, and gives employers a clear picture of what you can contribute. If you're a student looking for your first opportunity, a professional exploring a career change, or someone preparing for your next position, remember that you don't need to have every qualification already. You do need to understand what the role requires, recognize where your experience aligns, and take intentional steps to strengthen the areas where you're still developing.
